The Proliferation of LLMs in Cybersecurity

A Short Essay

The Arrival of Accessible AI in Cybersecurity

Claude Mythos made the news a few months ago as the first model with serious cybersecurity capabilities. Its full release was aborted, because it would lower the barrier for performing successful cyber attacks. However, LLMs are a technology made for a broad public. Keeping their full capabilities indefinitely inaccessible, would create a large power imbalance in favour of anyone who could get their hands on their full potential.

As recent developments suggest, the time for the proliferation of LLMs in cybersecurity has arrived. GLM 5.3, the best open weight model in cybersecurity, now has capabilities that are similar to Mythos. And techniques (such as changing the model weights with gradients meant to stop it from refusing unethical tasks) exist for cancelling its safeguards with only moderate resources. How impactful could this be for the internet? Could it be significant enough to shift the balance between offensive and defensive cybersecurity?

How does the Bigger Picture Change?

At a first glance, it may seem that it's just gonna be business as usual: attacking can be automated, penetration testing is also accelerated. Organisations of vital importance have had a window to protect themselves, that lasted several months (with Anthropic being the creator of this window). And such big organisations will have the capacity to keep auditing their systems and match any threats with adequate defensive capabilities. The only change would be an increase in the volume of potential attacks and thus an increase in defensive, AI-assisted activity. Therefore, maintaining a capable cybersecurity team would be of paramount importance and the cybersecurity costs (particularly new API costs) would have to increase.

However, this increase in cost would be universal. Every organisation could be hacked more easily, while many of them cannot afford to employ a serious cybersecurity team. Would someone bother to hack them, though? After all, it is already known that a lot of blatant vulnerabilities exist in the internet, but they are not exploited due to lack of significance. Different answers exist to this question...

Lower Barriers and Escalating Threats

One answer is that who can perform cyber attacks may change in the future. Until recently, a serious time commitment and significant practical experience was required before getting to hack any real system. Even the so-called "skiddies" needed to learn a lot of concepts, tools, etc. and their capabilities remained very limited. Now, people from more diverse backgrounds and less technical expertise may find their way into hacking real world systems. And many may find it an easy way to make money. Threatening to damage the public image of startups by publishing personal data they stored would be an example. Usual ransomware attacks could be another. If such "cyber petty-crime" against less protected targets has a lowered knowledge barrier, many people from unprivileged backgrounds may be tempted into it.

Another answer is that less defended targets could be used as entry points for access to higher profile organisations. A good example would be a public-sector organisation that uses outdated software. It may not have any intrinsic value as a target, but it could be a gateway for escalating attacks towards the relevant ministry. What the LLM enables, in this case, is not only the faster exploitation of such an entry point. In reality, it chiefly enables all the in between steps, from the low-profile entry point all the way to the final target. Each time the hacker unlocks new access (in terms of privileges, or new networks), the LLM can help to rapidly explore the new environment and find new exploits. Therefore, for the more capable hackers, what seems to change is that they can perform more sophisticated, multi-step attacks. Where previously the effort required may have been discouraging, the LLM now makes the task more easy. And such slow moving attacks could be particularly hard for most big organisations to counter, especially when they include social engineering as a component.

Conclusion: The Increasing Cost of Security

Maybe some threats can be countered and successfully constrained. For example, widespread honeypots could potentially stop the slowly propagating attacks and make them much less efficient. But, overall, it seems to me like the cost of security for online assets is going to increase. And economic damage is likely to amplify for anyone who doesn't have the resources to keep up.
